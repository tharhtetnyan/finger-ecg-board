## Overview

This project is a compact **electrocardiogram (ECG) acquisition PCB** developed as part of the Medical Electronic Design coursework at Soonchunhyang University.

The objective was to design and fabricate a biomedical analog front-end capable of acquiring low-amplitude cardiac biopotential signals from body electrodes, amplifying the differential signal, conditioning it through analog circuitry, and providing a real-time ECG waveform for oscilloscope observation.

The project covered the complete PCB development workflow using **OrCAD Capture and OrCAD PCB Editor**, including schematic design, component library management, footprint assignment, PCB placement and routing, ground configuration, drill generation, artwork preparation, and physical board fabrication.

## How It Works

### 1. ECG Signal Acquisition

Electrical activity generated by the heart produces small potential differences that can be detected at the surface of the body.

Two measurement electrodes detect this differential biopotential, while an additional reference/ground connection establishes the circuit reference.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    EP["Measurement Electrode +"] --> INA[INA126 Input]
    EN["Measurement Electrode −"] --> INA
    INA --> PROC[ECG Processing]
    PROC --> OUT[Analog Output]
    REF[Reference / Ground Electrode] --> REFC[Circuit Reference]
```

---

### 2. Instrumentation Amplification

The first major analog stage uses an **INA126 instrumentation amplifier**.

Biomedical signals such as ECG have relatively small amplitudes and can easily be affected by environmental interference. An instrumentation amplifier is useful for this application because it provides differential amplification while rejecting signals that appear simultaneously at both inputs.

Important characteristics include:

- High input impedance
- Differential signal amplification
- Common-mode signal rejection
- External gain configuration
- Suitability for low-amplitude sensor and biopotential signals

The INA126 therefore provides the interface between the body electrodes and the remaining analog signal-processing circuitry.

---

### 3. Analog Signal Processing

After the instrumentation-amplifier stage, the ECG signal is processed using **MCP6004-based operational-amplifier circuitry**.

The MCP6004 contains four operational amplifiers in a single package, allowing multiple analog functions to be integrated into the ECG board.

The analog stages provide the circuitry required for signal conditioning before the waveform reaches the output.

Potential analog processing functions include:

- Amplification
- Filtering
- Buffering
- Reference conditioning
- Output signal conditioning

---

## ECG Measurement Principle

An ECG does not measure an absolute electrical potential. Instead, it measures the **potential difference between electrodes placed at different locations on the body**.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    E1[Electrode 1] --> AMP["INA126<br/>Differential Amplifier"]
    E2[Electrode 2] --> AMP
    AMP --> OUT[Amplified ECG]
    REF[Reference Electrode] --> GND[Circuit Reference]
```

Signals common to both measurement electrodes can be suppressed by the differential front-end, while the voltage difference associated with cardiac activity is amplified.

## Why Use an Instrumentation Amplifier?

Biomedical signals introduce several challenges that make a conventional single operational-amplifier configuration less suitable as the primary input stage.

ECG signals are:

- Low amplitude
- Differential
- Sensitive to electromagnetic interference
- Acquired through electrode interfaces
- Susceptible to common-mode noise

An instrumentation amplifier provides a suitable front-end architecture because it combines **high input impedance, differential amplification, and common-mode rejection**.

This makes the instrumentation amplifier one of the most important components of the ECG acquisition system.

---

## Noise & Signal Integrity

One of the main practical challenges in ECG acquisition is extracting a useful physiological waveform from electrical noise.

Potential noise sources include:

- 50/60 Hz mains interference
- Electrode contact variation
- Motion artifacts
- Power-supply noise
- Long electrode wires
- Grounding problems
- Nearby electronic devices
- PCB trace coupling

The project demonstrated that successful biosignal acquisition depends not only on amplifier gain but also on the entire analog signal path, including electrode placement, grounding, filtering, PCB layout, and measurement setup.

## Testing & Experimental Result

The fabricated board was evaluated by connecting the electrode inputs to the body and recording the analog ECG output.

### Test Procedure

1. Connect the measurement electrodes to the body.
2. Connect the reference/ground electrode.
3. Power the ECG acquisition circuit.
4. Connect the analog output to the measurement system.
5. Record the ECG output in real time.
6. Evaluate waveform morphology and signal stability.
7. Check for excessive noise or interference.
